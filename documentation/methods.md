As illustrated in Table 1, selecting the optimal peak and valley detection method depends on both the characteristics of the remote sensing time series and the analysis objectives:

- **High-frequency noise (e.g., SAR backscatter):** Methods that explicitly address noise, such as wavelet transforms, Hilbert–Huang Transform (HHT), or machine learning models trained with noisy data, tend to perform best. Classical approaches may require strong smoothing, which risks obscuring true peaks. For example, detecting seasonal soil moisture peaks in Sentinel-1 SAR data often involves initial temporal filtering (e.g., median or wavelet-based) to reduce speckle, followed by peak detection on the smoothed series. Wavelet or EMD can help separate the seasonal signal from noise-dominated components.

- **Regular seasonal signals (e.g., NDVI of natural vegetation):** Simpler methods like Savitzky–Golay smoothing or STL decomposition are often sufficient, as these signals typically have a single, well-defined peak per year. Such methods have been validated in the literature for preserving peak timing and magnitude. Domain-specific curve fitting (e.g., asymmetric Gaussian models as in TIMESAT) can further improve accuracy for these cases.

- **Irregular or multi-modal patterns (e.g., multiple cropping seasons, mixed vegetation):** Flexible approaches such as wavelet analysis or EMD are advantageous, as they can capture multiple peaks or oscillations. Alternatively, peak-finding algorithms with adjustable window sizes (e.g., SciPy’s `find_peaks` with a small minimum distance) can be used after appropriate smoothing to detect closely spaced peaks, as in regions with double cropping.

- **Trend vs. transient peaks:** For detecting long-term changes or structural breaks (e.g., the historical peak and subsequent decline in a vegetation index), changepoint detection methods like BEAST or Bayesian approaches are more suitable. These methods can identify significant shifts in the time series, which differ from annual or cyclical peaks. For detecting peaks within each cycle, smoothing combined with local maxima detection remains the most direct approach.

- **Computational scalability:** Large-scale remote sensing analyses require efficient methods. Simpler algorithms (moving average, thresholding, Savitzky–Golay) can be applied across many pixels and parallelized easily. More computationally intensive methods (EMD, Bayesian changepoint detection) may be reserved for targeted analysis on selected pixels or downsampled data. Cloud platforms like Google Earth Engine (GEE) facilitate scalable application of simple methods, with more advanced analysis performed offline as needed. Deep learning approaches can leverage GPUs for parallel processing, but require substantial training data and resources.

- **Validation and literature support:** Numerous studies have validated these methods for specific applications. For example, combining seasonal-trend modeling with break detection (e.g., BFAST, LandTrendr) has improved forest disturbance detection (Zhu & Woodcock, 2014). Wavelet-based filtering has enhanced crop phenology extraction under noisy conditions (Sakamoto et al., 2005). EMD and HHT have been used to isolate drought impacts in NDVI time series by separating periodic and anomalous components (e.g., Vu et al., 2015). These examples highlight the importance of tailoring the method to the data and application—using break detection for disturbance, wavelet or curve fitting for agricultural peaks, and EMD/HHT for climate anomaly analysis.

In summary, method selection should be guided by the signal’s noise characteristics, periodicity, and the specific remote sensing question, with hybrid and validated approaches often yielding the most reliable results.

In conclusion, detecting peaks and valleys in remote sensing time series is a multi-faceted problem. Classical methods provide simplicity and speed and often do surprisingly well for regular signals; signal processing methods offer advanced noise-handling and multi-scale detection; statistical methods ensure robustness and interpretability in terms of significance; and machine learning methods bring flexibility to learn complex patterns. Often, the best solution is a hybrid approach (e.g., smooth with LOESS, then apply SciPy find_peaks, and finally verify significant changes with a Mann-Kendall trend test). The appropriate method depends on the nature of the data (noisy vs smooth, single peak vs multiple, stationary vs with trend) and the specific application in remote sensing (be it phenology, climate impact assessment, or land use change monitoring). By leveraging the rich set of tools and methods available – and cross-validating their outputs – analysts can reliably extract meaningful peaks and valleys, turning raw satellite observations into insights about environmental trends.
